# Ad Bible V1

This is the first compact, cross-genre foundation for the Ad Creative Factory. It guides research, strategy, art direction, production, review, and learning.

V1 contains 15 major principles for simplified testing. Genre, subgenre, format, platform, and game-specific knowledge remain in separate libraries and are retrieved only when relevant.

## Operating rules

1. Load this document for every mobile-game ad task.
2. Classify the game, genre, subgenre, format, audience, platform, and objective.
3. Retrieve only relevant playbooks and examples.
4. Treat examples as evidence, not instructions to copy.
5. Mark claims as verified, inferred, or unknown.
6. Add a new principle only when it creates a genuinely new cross-genre decision.

## Decision hierarchy

1. Verified game truth
2. Campaign objective
3. Platform and placement requirements
4. Ad Bible principles
5. Relevant genre and format playbooks
6. Performance evidence
7. Reference examples
8. Agent preference

## The 15 major principles

### 1. Make the creative promise clear quickly

Establish the game, player action, player desire, or unusual experience early enough for the viewer to understand the ad’s relevance. Fast clarity does not require fast editing.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 2. Show the core interaction early

When the mechanic is visually understandable, show the player input and immediate consequence near the beginning. Necessary story or transformation openings may delay this briefly, but interaction should follow quickly.

Scope: cross-genre, especially gameplay ads.

### 3. Build around one primary creative idea

Use one dominant promise, tension, fantasy, or emotional reason to continue watching. Secondary details must support it rather than compete with it.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 4. Make cause and effect visible

Make the loop legible: Input → consequence → progress, failure, reward, or change. The consequence must be understandable.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 5. Choose a deliberate emotional arc

Choose the viewer journey before selecting visual treatment. Examples include curiosity to satisfaction, challenge to mastery, calm to completion, desire to reward, and surprise to excitement.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 6. Make progress or change visible

Show development through clearing, growth, resources, territory, upgrades, defeat, collection, or increasing difficulty. Slow games may use subtle progress, but it must remain perceptible.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 7. Use truthful game evidence

Build around verified mechanics, visuals, outcomes, characters, features, and claims. Never invent unavailable gameplay or imply unsupported rewards. Adapt a source ad’s emotional mechanism rather than copying a false feature.

Scope: cross-genre; mandatory.

### 8. Prioritize mobile readability

Review first-second comprehension, text size, contrast, focal point, safe areas, UI scale, clutter, and crop behavior at phone size or in an equivalent ad preview.

Scope: cross-genre mobile ads.

### 9. Turn the mechanic into visual identity

Make the central mechanic distinctive through shape, material, color, motion, character, environment, or transformation. The identity must support the game, not decorate unrelated gameplay.

Scope: cross-genre; expression is genre-specific.

### 10. Choose the right tension level

Select the emotional strategy that fits the game and audience: challenge, relaxation, surprise, power, humor, competition, status, or discovery. The Creative Strategist chooses the territory; the Art Director executes it coherently.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 11. Give the viewer a reason to imagine playing

Create a personal response such as “I can solve that”, “I want to try that move”, “I want to see what happens”, “I need that satisfying feeling”, or “I want to become stronger”. Keep the imagined action connected to the real game.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 12. Use controlled variation

Every variant should test a meaningful difference while preserving enough constant information to interpret the result. Possible axes include hook, territory, audience angle, pacing, gameplay moment, visual identity, creator, script, CTA, or sound.

Scope: cross-genre.

### 13. Use sound and motion as part of the idea

Motion, timing, interaction feedback, and sound should reinforce attention, understanding, emotion, or reward. Do not add them merely to fill empty space.

Scope: cross-genre; expression is format-specific.

### 14. Preserve originality and provenance

Extract transferable mechanisms from references without reproducing exact footage, scripts, characters, recognizable likenesses, trademarks, distinctive living-artist styles, or unique compositions. Maintain source and usage records; unknown provenance remains unknown.

Scope: cross-genre; mandatory.

### 15. Learn without overgeneralizing

Keep observations, patterns, principles, hypotheses, performance learnings, and rejection reasons separate. A single successful ad is not a universal rule. Increase confidence only when comparable evidence repeats.

Scope: cross-genre; mandatory.

## Anti-repetition rule

Before creating a new principle, compare it with all existing principles. Merge it when it has the same meaning, decision impact, scope, and category. Create a separate pattern when it is a genre, subgenre, format, audience, or platform-specific expression of an existing principle.

## V1 evidence status

The current evidence base includes Arrow-family and block/color puzzle ad analyses. These support useful observations but do not establish causal performance claims because campaign metrics have not yet been attached.
